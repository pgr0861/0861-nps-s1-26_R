Background research

Computing and Its Carbon Footprint · August 2026

1. Background

It’s easy to forget that computing has a physical footprint at all. Every search, stream, or AI-generated image is running on a data center somewhere, and those data centers eat up electricity, water, and raw materials at a rate that’s no longer trivial (Stax Engineering, 2025). A decade ago this might have seemed like a minor side-effect of modern life, something not worth thinking too hard about. That’s changed. As more of daily life moves online and AI tools become part of everyday routines, the infrastructure behind them keeps expanding, and so does its cost. But this isn’t just about electricity used while something is running. A surprising amount of computing’s carbon output actually comes from building the hardware in the first place, not from using it (Gupta et al., 2021), which complicates the usual story people tell about “clean” computing. This paper looks at that footprint from a few angles: what gets emitted during production, what gets emitted through everyday use and AI training, and why the whole thing is so hard to actually measure.

2. Emissions from production

When people picture computing’s environmental impact, they usually think of electricity: a laptop plugged in overnight, or a data center humming away 24/7. But a lot of the damage is already done before a device is even switched on. Building a chip, a phone, or a server means mining raw materials, refining them, and running factories that are themselves energy-intensive, often across multiple countries before a single component reaches final assembly. In fact, research suggests this manufacturing stage often produces more emissions than the device will over its entire working life (Gupta et al., 2021). This is especially true for smaller devices like phones and laptops, since people tend to replace them fairly often, sometimes every couple of years, which means starting the whole manufacturing process over again rather than simply keeping something running longer. So even a computer running on 100% renewable electricity isn’t carbon-neutral, not really; it was never that clean to begin with. Clean energy alone doesn’t fix this problem. Production deserves just as much scrutiny as usage does, if not more, given how easy it is to overlook.

3. Emissions from AI training and inference

Before getting into AI specifically, it’s worth remembering that computing’s day-to-day electricity use is already substantial on its own. Data centers were responsible for 1.5% of electricity use around the world in 2024, and that number is expected to more than double by the year 2030 (Spowart, 2026). Some of that growth is generic, driven by streaming, cloud storage, and everyday browsing, but a lot of it is increasingly tied to AI, which helps explain why one recent estimate put data center emissions at 286 million tons of CO2 in 2025 alone (Zinin, 2026).

Training the large AI models behind tools like chatbots and image generators is one of the more energy-hungry corners of modern computing. Before a model can do anything useful, it has to be trained on enormous amounts of data, often for weeks at a time across thousands of processors running in parallel, all of which draw significant power the entire time. One estimate put the cost of training GPT-3, a 175-billion-parameter model, at around 1,287 megawatt-hours of electricity and roughly 502 metric tons of carbon, about the same as driving 112 petrol cars for a year (State of the Planet, 2023). What’s less obvious is that training isn’t even the bigger half of the problem. Once a model is actually released, it has to answer millions of queries every day, and this “inference” stage can end up burning through more energy overall than training ever did (State of the Planet, 2023). That’s a hard thing to picture at first, since a single question typed into a chatbot feels weightless, but multiplied across millions of users asking questions every hour, it adds up fast. In other words, the environmental cost doesn’t stop once the model is finished; if anything, it just keeps compounding the more popular the tool becomes.

4. Difficulty in measuring data

There is, however, a small problem, no one is entirely sure of the real number. Companies are not always transparent about how much electricity their data centers draw or where that power actually comes from, so researchers are often left estimating rather than measuring directly (Lannelongue & Inouye, 2023). On top of that, studies don’t agree on what counts. Some only tally the electricity used mid-task; others fold in manufacturing, cooling, or the energy wasted while hardware just sits idle (Gupta et al., 2021). Even the assumptions behind these estimates, like which power grid a data center draws from, or how efficiently a specific facility is run, can shift the final number quite a bit. That’s a big part of why you’ll see wildly different figures for something as specific as training one AI model. Without more consistent reporting, it’s hard to say how big this problem really is, let alone whether current efforts to shrink it are working.

5. Conclusion

Computing’s carbon footprint turns out to be a lot messier than it first looks. It’s not just the electricity running through devices and data centers; it’s the manufacturing behind the hardware, and the training and daily use of AI systems on top of that. None of these pieces can really be solved on their own, and treating “switch to renewables” as a fix-all misses most of the picture. Getting a fuller picture probably means better reporting from the companies running this infrastructure, and a bit more awareness from everyone else about what’s actually happening behind the screen. As computing, and AI in particular, keeps growing, getting a clearer and more honest picture of its impact is only going to matter more.
