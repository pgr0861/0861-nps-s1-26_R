Cameron Taylor & Grace Girardi

Topic
Greenhouse gas emissions and carbon accounting
Position / organisation
Swinburne Sustainability Team (Operations)
Date
10 August 2026

Speaker background

Cameron is the Sustainability Program Manager at Swinburne, leading integrated sustainability strategy and projects. He previously worked at places such as JLL, Treasury and Finance Victoria, and the City of Whittlesea.

Grace is a Sustainability Analyst at Swinburne. She interprets data from Swinburne’s carbon footprint and offsets, and has a deep passion for environmental science, having completed a Master’s.

Key ideas and messages

  1. Carbon accounting is quite a messy and imprecise thing. Even with two decimal points of reporting, a lot of the data is actually based on estimates or third-party calculation methods that are not public.
  2. National and organisational reporting are two distinct systems. Governments are under systems like the Paris Agreement, whereas organisations report separately.
  3. Scopes 1, 2 and 3 are an important framework for carbon accounting.

Questions

How do you decide what actually counts as Scope 3, versus something you just don’t track at all? (I asked this in person.)

Implications

My project
The Scope 1/2/3 framework as a structure for analysing my project details.
My career
Grace’s personal story was interesting to hear; a hybrid of skills is something I would be interested in pursuing.
Other
Personally developing a healthy scepticism towards “clean energy” numbers.

Callum Jones

Topic
Electricity’s role in Australia’s decarbonisation
Position / organisation
Engineer, AusNet
Date
25 August 2026

Speaker background

His first full-time role out of university is at AusNet. He also completed a final-year project at RMIT on electric vehicle uptake.

Key ideas and messages

  1. Grid frequency (Australia runs at 50 Hz) reflects the balance of supply and demand. Traditional spinning generators provide “inertia” that stabilises the grid, while inverter-based renewables provide little to none.
  2. Both extremes are a problem: too much demand (extreme heat or cold, the “duck curve”) and too little demand (mild weather, high midday solar) each require different tools.
  3. Transmission stays essential even as generation decentralises, because of geography (generation is often far from load), the ability to share resources across regions, economic competition among generators, and resilience during faults.

Questions

What happens if two neighbouring states both hit minimum demand at the same time and can’t export power to each other?

Implications

My project
Use the transmission vs distribution distinction to explain why some areas lose power while others don’t during an outage.
My career
It gave me a clearer picture of what day-to-day work in network operations and planning actually looks like.
Other
It made blackouts and load shedding easier to explain to someone without an engineering background, using the “freeway vs road” analogy.

Anna Boyne

Topic
Electricity’s role in Australia’s decarbonisation
Position / organisation
AEMO (Australian Energy Market Operator)
Date
25 August 2026

Speaker background

About 25 years working across government projects and utilities, with a background in geomatics and a PhD on uncertainty in data and how people reason with it. More recently she spent time at an NGO working on climate action before returning to the electricity sector.

Key ideas and messages

  1. There’s an important distinction between “energy” (the actual amount of electricity delivered over time, like water filling a bucket) and “capacity” (the maximum rate at which a generator can produce, like how fast the bucket can be filled).
  2. The electricity “system” (engineering-focused: keeping voltage and frequency stable, minimising blackouts) is distinct from the “market” (financial: fair pricing, viable business models for generators and retailers, avoiding price volatility).
  3. Managing periods of too much renewable supply (e.g. midday solar) is a growing challenge alongside managing peak demand. It’s addressed through storage, state-based market mechanisms, and non-market interventions when needed.

Questions

How exactly do the “non-market interventions” work when a state has too much or too little demand, and who decides when to use them instead of market mechanisms?

Implications

My project
Remember to check whether a dataset is measuring energy or capacity before drawing conclusions from it.
My career
It shows that a data or geomatics background can lead into the energy sector, not just a traditional engineering path.
Other
It made me want to look more closely at how “100% renewable” claims are actually calculated before taking them at face value.

Jonathan Howe

Topic
Melbourne’s climate and weather patterns
Position / organisation
Media and Communications, Bureau of Meteorology
Date
31 August 2026

Speaker background

He did a Bachelor of Commerce and Science at Monash, majoring in atmospheric science and mathematics, plus economics with a focus on climate change economics. He worked in economics consulting for two years before joining the Bureau 13 years ago.

Key ideas and messages

  1. Melbourne’s climate is highly variable from day to day (“four seasons in one day”) because the city sits between a hot desert to the north and the cold Southern Ocean to the south, so any change in wind direction causes rapid temperature swings.
  2. Historical weather station data has quality and consistency issues to be aware of. For example, Melbourne’s “official” record moved from Lonsdale Street to Olympic Park in 2013 because of urban heat effects, so it matters which station’s data you use.
  3. Major weather disasters (e.g. Black Saturday, the 2022 Maribyrnong floods and the 2016 thunderstorm asthma event) have historically driven real policy and warning-system changes.

Questions

How do the different global forecasting models (ACCESS, European, US, etc.) end up disagreeing so much on the same forecast period, and how does the Bureau decide which one to lean on in a given situation? (I asked this in person.)

Implications

My project
When analysing historical climate or weather trends, check which weather station the data comes from, and be aware that pre-2015 Melbourne data may require the “closed stations” archive (Lonsdale Street) rather than the current Olympic Park site.
My career
It shows a career path that blends science (meteorology and climate) with communication skills (media, content creation, public engagement).
Other
A good reminder to question anomalies in data (e.g. an unusually hot or cold year) rather than taking them at face value; there’s often a real climatic cause (El Niño, a volcanic eruption, etc.) behind a spike.

Reflection

SSituation

Over August, seven external guest speakers presented to our class, covering carbon accounting at Swinburne, Melbourne’s weather, the electricity grid and energy market, equity in STEM, and science communication.

TTask

I needed to take notes on each talk using the unit’s guest speaker template: each speaker’s background, their key ideas, my questions, and what the talk meant for my project, my career and beyond.

AAction

I took notes on every talk and asked two of the speakers my questions in person: Cameron and Grace about Scope 3, and Jonathan Howe about forecasting models. The more technical talks, on the electricity grid and market, were dense and took effort to follow.

RResult

The talks I found most useful were Cameron and Grace’s on carbon accounting and Jonathan Howe’s on Melbourne’s weather. They helped with our project, through the Scope 1/2/3 framework and checking which weather station our data comes from, and they gave me career ideas, since many of the speakers blend science with communication or data.

What I’d do differently / next

Next time, I’d follow up with speakers afterwards, for example by connecting with them on LinkedIn.
