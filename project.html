Project: Final Project

Our group project, Viridus Machina: could algae help Swinburne capture its own carbon?

Our project video

Watch “Photobioreactors for Carbon Insetting” on YouTube

Overview

The problem

In 2019, Swinburne declared a climate emergency and committed to becoming carbon neutral by 2025. It has cut its emissions by about 90%, mostly by switching to renewable electricity, but some emissions are left over, like the natural gas burned to heat its buildings. Those are currently balanced out with carbon offsets: paying for projects somewhere else. Our project looked at a different idea, called “insetting”: cutting carbon on campus instead.

Our idea

Algae such as Chlorella vulgaris absorb CO2 through photosynthesis much faster than trees, multiply quickly, and can be harvested and turned into fuel. Most of the gas Swinburne uses goes into boilers that heat its buildings, and their exhaust is warm and full of CO2. We proposed sending that exhaust through a photobioreactor, a glass tank of algae, so the algae capture the CO2 and get the warmth they need. The algae could then be turned into biogas for the same boilers, replacing some of the gas Swinburne buys: carbon captured, fuel made, less gas burned. We also looked at smaller indoor units that pull CO2 out of the air in a room.

The challenges

Melbourne’s weather is a problem for the algae. Our solar data from the Scotch College weather station showed that from late April to mid-August, almost every day falls below the light Chlorella vulgaris needs to grow well, and Melbourne’s daily average temperature almost never reaches the 25–30 °C it prefers. A rooftop tank would need LED lighting and a source of warmth, which is why pairing it with the boilers made sense. Cost is the other challenge: the BIQ house in Hamburg, covered in 129 algae panels since 2013, cost around $3,330 per square metre, while the target for Australian projects is closer to $350.

Next steps

Our next step would be asking Swinburne’s facilities team which buildings and boilers would be the best fit, and looking into whether captured carbon could earn carbon credits to help pay for the system.

My contribution

My roles were data and analysis, editing our video, and writing parts of the script. My solar exposure spreadsheet analysed 244 days of Bureau of Meteorology data from the Scotch College weather station, and it’s what showed that Melbourne’s winter light is too low for the algae without LED lighting.
